1 · Two ways of thinking about motion

For ~2000 years Aristotle taught that a moving object naturally wants to stop, and that Earth must sit still at the center — because we feel no motion.

Then Galileo ran experiments. Rolling balls on ramps, he reasoned that a ball wants to keep going and only slows because of friction. Remove friction → motion never stops on its own. That's the seed of inertia.

  Release here                       Rises to (almost)
        \                              the same height
         \____________________________/
                (level stretch)

Make the second ramp flatter and the ball rolls farther to reach the same height. Flatten it fully and — in a frictionless world — it rolls forever.

💡 Big idea: a moving object doesn't need a force to keep moving. It needs a force only to change its motion.
Q1 · Concept
Aristotle thought Earth was at rest because…a) we feel no sensation of a moving Earth · b) Earth rotates · c) perfect circle · d) elliptical path

Answer: a. We feel no sensation of motion, so it seemed Earth was still. Smooth, steady motion is undetectable from the inside.

Q2 · Concept
Science advanced when Galileo favored…a) philosophy over experiment · b) experiment over philosophy · c) nonmathematical thinking · d) none

Answer: b. His revolution was testing ideas by experiment instead of just debating them.

Q3 · Concept
Roll a ball on a level surface and it would…a) slow to rest naturally · b) keep rolling if no friction · c) roll as long as inertia nudges it · d) reverse

Answer: b. With no friction, nothing removes its motion. (c) is a trap — inertia doesn't nudge; it isn't a force.

Q4 · Concept
Down one incline, up another — the ball rolled…a) almost to its initial height · b) halfway · c) to its original height · d) higher

Answer: a. In the real experiment it reaches almost its start; the shortfall is friction. The frictionless idealization ("to its original height") is what reveals inertia.

2 · Inertia: the lazy property of matter

Inertia is the resistance of any object to a change in its motion. At rest → wants to stay at rest. Moving → wants to keep moving straight at constant speed. More mass → more inertia (a loaded truck vs. a bicycle).

⚠️ Top misconception: inertia is not a force. It's a property every chunk of matter has. Nothing "pushes" with inertia.
Q5 · Concept
According to Galileo, inertia is a…a) force like any other · b) special force · c) property of all matter · d) opposite of force

Answer: c. A property of all matter. If you picked "force," re-read the warning above.

3 · Forces, vectors & the net force

A force is a push or pull. A vector needs both size and direction.

QuantityVector?Why
Speed❌ NoMagnitude only ("50 km/h")
Velocity✅ YesSpeed + direction
Force✅ YesStrength + direction

The net force = the single force with the same effect as all of them. Same direction → add. Opposite → subtract, pointing the way of the bigger one.

  Girl 100 N ◀──────●────▶ Boy 50 N
        Net = 100 − 50 = 50 N to the LEFT
Q6 · Concept
Which is NOT a vector?a) Speed · b) Velocity · c) Force · d) all are vectors

Answer: a. Speed is just a number; velocity and force carry direction.

Q7 · Concept
Girl pushes left 100 N, boy right 50 N. Net force?a) 100 N left · b) 100 N right · c) 50 N left · d) 50 N right

Answer: c. 100 − 50 = 50 N to the left (toward the stronger push).

Q8 · Concept
10-kg block: 20 N east + 15 N west. Combined force?a) 35 N west · b) 35 N east · c) 5 N east · d) 5 N west

Answer: c. 20 − 15 = 5 N east. The 10 kg is a distractor — you don't need mass for the net force.

4 · Newton's First Law in action

Newton's First Law (Law of Inertia): every object continues at rest, or in uniform straight-line motion, unless acted on by a net force.

Remove the net force and motion doesn't fade — it coasts. Take away the force that curves a path, and the object flies off straight.

  • Tablecloth trick: dishes stay put — inertia keeps them at rest while the cloth slips out.
  • Ball in a jerked cart: the ball "stays behind," so the back wall catches up to it.
  • Space probe: no force acting, yet it keeps cruising.
  • Earth & Sun: if gravity vanished, Earth shoots off in a straight line.
  • Jump by a wall: you both already share Earth's ~30 km/s, so nothing slams into you.
Q9 · Concept
Dishes stay as you yank the cloth — illustrating…a) friction · b) inertia · c) constant motion · d) ΣF = 0

Answer: b. Inertia — the dishes resist a change from rest.

Q10 · Concept
If Sun–Earth gravity vanished, Earth would move in…a) curved path · b) outward spiral · c) inward spiral · d) straight line

Answer: d. No force to bend the path → a straight line.

Q11 · Concept
A space probe in deep space keeps moving…a) because a force acts · b) in a curve · c) even though no force acts · d) due to gravity

Answer: c. No force is needed to keep moving.

Q12 · Concept
Ball in the middle of a cart; jerk the cart forward. The ball…a) hits the front · b) hits the back · c) stays in the middle · d) depends

Answer: b. The ball tends to stay at rest; the cart shoots forward, so the back wall meets the ball.

Q13 · Concept
Earth moves ~30 km/s; jump by a wall and it doesn't hit you because the wall…a) too little gravity · b) moves opposite · c) shares your horizontal speed throughout · d) negligible inertia

Answer: c. You and the wall share the same horizontal motion the whole time — exactly why Earth's motion is unfelt.

5 · Equilibrium: when forces cancel (ΣF = 0)

When forces add to zero, the object is in equilibrium. ΣF means "sum of all forces."

Equilibrium rule: ΣF = 0. Holds for anything at rest and anything at constant straight-line velocity. Handy form: total up = total down.

Support force: a 10 N book on a table → the table pushes up exactly 10 N. Tension: a 10 N weight on two even strands → 5 N each.

  Tension(L) ▲                  ▲ Tension(R)
             └──[ scaffold + painters ]──┘
                        │ weight ▼
   Tension(L) + Tension(R) = total weight  (up = down)

Painters on opposite ends → tensions depend on their weights. One painter (or a piggy-back pair) in the exact middle → tensions are equal by symmetry.

Q14 · Concept
10-N object on two vertical strands — tension in each?a) <5 N · b) 5 N · c) >5 N · d) 10 N

Answer: b. Shared evenly → 5 N each (5 + 5 = 10).

Q15 · Concept
Burl & Paul on opposite ends — the two rope tensions…a) are equal · b) depend on their relative weights · c) add to zero · d) are in equilibrium

Answer: b. The rope nearer the heavier painter carries more — equal only if they weigh the same.

Q16 · Concept
Burl carries Paul piggy-back in the middle — tensions…a) cancel to zero · b) be equal · c) be unequal · d) support more easily

Answer: b. Dead center = symmetric load → equal tensions.

Q17 · Concept
Burl + Paul = 1300 N; rope tensions add to 1700 N. Scaffold weighs…a) 400 N · b) 500 N · c) 600 N · d) 800 N

Answer: a. Up = down: 1700 supports painters + scaffold → scaffold = 1700 − 1300 = 400 N.

Q18 · Concept
10-N book on a table — support force is…a) <10 N · b) 10 N · c) >10 N · d) depends on orientation

Answer: b. At rest, the table pushes up exactly 10 N.

Q19 · Concept
ΣF = 0 applies to…a) objects at rest · b) objects in uniform straight-line motion · c) both · d) neither

Answer: c. Both — "nothing changing" covers rest and constant velocity.

Q20 · Concept
The net force on any object in equilibrium is…a) zero · b) 10 m/s² · c) equal to its weight · d) a bit less than weight

Answer: a. That's the definition — net force = zero.

6 · 🛠️ Worked Examples

Example 1 · Equilibrium
Scales read 700 N and 500 N. Burl weighs 600 N and carries 100 N of paint. What does the scaffold weigh? 
  700 N ▲                       ▲ 500 N
        └──[ Burl 600 + paint 100 ]──┘

At rest → total up = total down.

1. Up = 700 + 500 = 1200 N → so total weight = 1200 N.
2. Burl + paint = 700 N.
3. Scaffold = 1200 − 700 = 500 N. ✅

Example 2 · Inertia
A pellet is fired through a flat spiral tube. Leaving the open end, which path: A (keeps curving), B (straight), or C? (ignore gravity)

Inside, the wall forces it to curve. The instant it exits — no wall, no force — so by the law of inertia it travels in a straight line.

Answer: B — straight line. (A is the classic wrong guess: the pellet doesn't "remember" the curve.)

Example 3 · Projectile
A ball swings on a string; at the lowest point (moving horizontally) a razor cuts the string. Which path next?

Split the motion at the cut:

1. Horizontal: no horizontal force → keeps constant horizontal speed (inertia).
2. Vertical: gravity pulls it down → it accelerates downward.

Constant sideways speed + speeding-up fall = a parabola.

Answer: (b) — a parabolic path, like any projectile.

Example 4 · Reference frames
You float down a river. Two life preservers: one 3 m upstream, one 3 m downstream. Swim to which first for the shortest time?

You, the water, and both preservers all drift together. Relative to the water it's just like a still pool — both preservers sit 3 m away.

Answer: (c) — both take the same time. The river's motion carries everyone equally and cancels out (same logic as jumping by a wall, Q13).
