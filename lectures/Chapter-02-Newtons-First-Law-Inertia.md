## 1. Two ways of thinking about motion: Aristotle vs. Galileo

### The story
For ~2000 years, **Aristotle's** idea ruled: he sorted motion into "natural" and "violent," and believed a moving object naturally *wants to stop*. He also thought Earth must be standing still at the center of the universe — because we *feel* no motion.

Then **Galileo** changed the game. Instead of arguing from philosophy, he **experimented**. Rolling balls down and up ramps, he reasoned that a ball *wants to keep going* — it only slows because of **friction**. Remove friction, and motion never stops on its own. This idea is the seed of **inertia**.

### Galileo's ramp experiment (the key insight) 🔑

```
   Release here                          Rises to (almost)
        \                                   the same height
         \                                       /
          \_____________________________________/
                    (level stretch)
```

Roll a ball down one ramp and up another: it climbs back to **almost** its starting height (a little is lost to friction). Now make the second ramp *less and less steep*. The ball must roll *farther* to reach that same height. **Flatten it completely**, and the ball — forever chasing a height it can never reach — would, in a frictionless world, **roll forever**.

> 💡 **Big idea:** A moving object doesn't need a force to *keep* moving. It needs a force only to *change* its motion.

### 🧠 Concept Checks

<details>
<summary><b>Q1.</b> Aristotle and his followers thought Earth was at rest at the center of the universe because… (a) humans feel no sensation of a moving Earth · (b) Earth rotates on its axis · (c) Earth moves in a perfect circle · (d) Earth moves in an ellipse</summary>

> **Answer: (a)** — We feel no sensation of motion, so it *seemed* obvious Earth was still. (Smooth, steady motion is undetectable from the inside — a theme we'll revisit.)
</details>

<details>
<summary><b>Q2.</b> Science greatly advanced when Galileo favored… (a) philosophy over experiment · (b) experiment over philosophy · (c) nonmathematical thinking · (d) none of these</summary>

> **Answer: (b)** — Galileo's revolution was **testing ideas by experiment** instead of just debating them.
</details>

<details>
<summary><b>Q3.</b> Galileo said that if you rolled a ball along a level surface, it would… (a) slow down due to a natural tendency to rest · (b) keep rolling without slowing if no friction acted · (c) roll as long as inertia nudged it along · (d) soon reverse direction</summary>

> **Answer: (b)** — With **no friction**, nothing removes its motion, so it keeps rolling. (Note "c" is a trap: inertia doesn't *nudge* anything — it isn't a force.)
</details>

<details>
<summary><b>Q4.</b> Rolling a ball down one incline so it rolls up another, Galileo found the ball rolled… (a) almost to its initial height · (b) halfway up · (c) to its original height · (d) higher than its start</summary>

> **Answer: (a)** — In the *real* experiment it reaches **almost** its starting height; the small shortfall is **friction**. (The idealized, frictionless conclusion is "to its original height" — that idealization is what reveals inertia.)
</details>

## 2. Inertia: the lazy property of matter

**Inertia** is the resistance of any object to a *change* in its state of motion.

- A resting object "wants" to stay at rest.
- A moving object "wants" to keep moving in a straight line at constant speed.

The more **mass** an object has, the more inertia it has — a loaded truck is far harder to start or stop than a bicycle.

> ⚠️ **Common trap:** Inertia is **not a force**. It's a *property* that every chunk of matter has. Nothing "pushes" with inertia.

### 🧠 Concept Check

<details>
<summary><b>Q5.</b> According to Galileo, inertia is a… (a) force like any other · (b) special kind of force · (c) property of all matter · (d) concept opposite to force</summary>

> **Answer: (c)** — Inertia is a **property of all matter**. If you picked a "force," re-read the warning above — that's the #1 misconception in this chapter.
</details>

## 3. Forces, vectors, and the net force

A **force** is a push or a pull. To work with forces you need two facts about each one: **how big** (magnitude) and **which way** (direction). A quantity that needs *both* is a **vector**.

| Quantity | Vector? | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Speed | ❌ No | Magnitude only ("50 km/h") |
| Velocity | ✅ Yes | Speed **+ direction** ("50 km/h north") |
| Force | ✅ Yes | Strength **+ direction** |

### Combining forces → the net force
The **net force** is the single force that has the same effect as all the forces combined.
- **Same direction:** add them.
- **Opposite directions:** subtract; the net points the way of the bigger force.

```
   Girl pulls 100 N  ◀────────────●────▶  Boy pulls 50 N
                                   │
                     Net = 100 − 50 = 50 N to the LEFT
```

### 🧠 Concept Checks

<details>
<summary><b>Q6.</b> Which of these is <b>NOT</b> a vector quantity? (a) Speed · (b) Velocity · (c) Force · (d) All are vectors</summary>

> **Answer: (a)** — **Speed** is just a number. Velocity and force both carry a direction, so they're vectors.
</details>

<details>
<summary><b>Q7.</b> A girl pushes a cart left with 100 N; a boy pushes it right with 50 N. The net force is… (a) 100 N left · (b) 100 N right · (c) 50 N left · (d) 50 N right</summary>

> **Answer: (c)** — Opposite directions subtract: 100 − 50 = **50 N to the left** (toward the stronger push).
</details>

<details>
<summary><b>Q8.</b> A 10-kg block is pushed eastward with 20 N and westward with 15 N. The combined force is… (a) 35 N west · (b) 35 N east · (c) 5 N east · (d) 5 N west</summary>

> **Answer: (c)** — 20 N east − 15 N west = **5 N east**. (The mass, 10 kg, is a distractor — you don't need it for the net force.)
</details>

## 4. Newton's First Law in action

> **Newton's First Law (the Law of Inertia):**
> *Every object continues in a state of rest, or of uniform motion in a straight line, unless acted upon by a **net** force.*

Two halves, one idea:

- **At rest** → stays at rest.
- **In motion** → keeps the *same speed* in a *straight line*.

Remove the net force and motion doesn't fade away — it **coasts**. Curve a moving object's path only by applying a force; take the force away and the object flies off **straight**.

### Everyday and cosmic examples

- **Tablecloth trick:** yank the cloth fast and the dishes stay put — their inertia keeps them at rest while the cloth slips away.
- **Ball in a jerked cart:** the cart lurches forward, but the ball "stays behind," so the **back of the cart catches up to it**.
- **Space probe:** far from everything, with *no* force acting, it just keeps cruising.
- **Earth & Sun:** if gravity vanished, Earth wouldn't spiral — it would shoot off in a **straight line**, like a ball let go from a sling.
- **Jumping by a wall:** you and the wall already share Earth's ~30 km/s motion, so the wall doesn't slam into you mid-jump — you're both carried along together (echoing Aristotle's puzzle in Q1).

### 🧠 Concept Checks

<details>
<summary><b>Q9.</b> When dishes stay put as you yank the tablecloth away, you're illustrating… (a) friction · (b) inertia · (c) constant motion · (d) ΣF = 0</summary>

> **Answer: (b)** — **Inertia**: the dishes resist a change from rest, so they stay while the cloth slides out.
</details>

<details>
<summary><b>Q10.</b> If gravity between the Sun and Earth suddenly vanished, Earth would move in… (a) a curved path · (b) an outward spiral · (c) an inward spiral · (d) a straight line</summary>

> **Answer: (d)** — With no force to bend its path, Earth obeys inertia and travels in a **straight line**.
</details>

<details>
<summary><b>Q11.</b> A space probe in remote outer space continues moving… (a) because a force acts on it · (b) in a curved path · (c) even though no force acts on it · (d) due to gravity</summary>

> **Answer: (c)** — No force is needed to *keep* moving. Motion continues **even though no force acts**.
</details>

<details>
<summary><b>Q12.</b> A ball rests in the middle of a cart. You quickly jerk the cart forward. The ball… (a) hits the front · (b) hits the back · (c) stays in the middle · (d) depends on the pull</summary>

> **Answer: (b)** — The ball tends to **stay at rest**; the cart shoots forward, so the **back wall comes to meet the ball**. Relative to the cart, the ball "slides backward."
</details>

<details>
<summary><b>Q13.</b> Earth moves ~30 km/s around the Sun. When you jump up beside a wall, it doesn't slam into you at 30 km/s because the wall… (a) has too little gravity · (b) moves opposite to you · (c) shares your horizontal speed before, during, and after the jump · (d) has negligible inertia</summary>

> **Answer: (c)** — You and the wall **share the same horizontal motion** the whole time, so there's no relative slam. This is exactly why Aristotle's contemporaries felt no "moving Earth."
</details>

## 5. Equilibrium: when forces cancel (ΣF = 0)

When the forces on something **add up to zero**, it's in **mechanical equilibrium**. The symbol ΣF means "the sum of all forces."

> **The Equilibrium Rule:** **ΣF = 0**
> This holds for anything **at rest** *and* anything moving at **constant velocity in a straight line** — in both cases, *nothing is changing*.

A handy way to use it: **(total upward force) = (total downward force)**.

### Support force (normal force) and tension

- Set a 10 N book on a table: it doesn't move, so the table must push **up** with exactly **10 N** to balance gravity. That upward push is the **support (normal) force**.
- Hang a 10 N weight from **two** vertical strands: together they supply 10 N up, so each carries **5 N** (when shared evenly).

### Scaffold logic (the painter problems)

A scaffold hangs from two ropes. The rope **tensions** add up to support *everything* on the scaffold:

```
   Rope tension (left)            Rope tension (right)
        ▲                                ▲
        │   ┌───────────────────────┐    │
        └───┤   scaffold + painters  ├───┘
            └───────────────────────┘
                      │
                      ▼  total weight (down)

      Tension_left + Tension_right  =  total weight (up = down)
```

- Painters on **opposite ends**: each rope carries more of the painter standing nearer to it, so tensions **depend on their relative weights** (equal only if the painters weigh the same).
- One painter (or a pigg-back pair) standing in the **exact middle**: by symmetry the two tensions are **equal**.

### 🧠 Concept Checks

<details>
<summary><b>Q14.</b> A 10-N object hangs at rest from two vertical strands of rope. The tension in each is… (a) slightly less than 5 N · (b) 5 N · (c) slightly more than 5 N · (d) 10 N</summary>

> **Answer: (b)** — Two vertical strands share the 10 N load equally → **5 N each** (5 + 5 = 10 up = 10 down).
</details>

<details>
<summary><b>Q15.</b> Painters Burl and Paul stand on opposite ends of a scaffold. The two rope tensions… (a) are equal · (b) depend on their relative weights · (c) add to zero · (d) are in equilibrium</summary>

> **Answer: (b)** — Standing apart, the rope nearer the heavier painter carries more, so the tensions **depend on their relative weights**. (They're equal *only* if Burl and Paul weigh the same.)
</details>

<details>
<summary><b>Q16.</b> If Burl carries Paul piggy-back while standing in the <b>middle</b> of the scaffold, the two rope tensions would… (a) cancel to zero · (b) be equal · (c) be unequal · (d) support them more easily</summary>

> **Answer: (b)** — Dead center means the load is symmetric, so the tensions are **equal**.
</details>

<details>
<summary><b>Q17.</b> Burl and Paul together weigh 1300 N. The two rope tensions add to 1700 N. The scaffold itself must weigh… (a) 400 N · (b) 500 N · (c) 600 N · (d) 800 N</summary>

> **Answer: (a)** — Total up = total down: 1700 N supports the painters *plus* the scaffold. Scaffold = 1700 − 1300 = **400 N**.
</details>

<details>
<summary><b>Q18.</b> Place a 10-N book on a table. The support force on the book is… (a) slightly less than 10 N · (b) 10 N · (c) slightly more than 10 N · (d) depends on orientation</summary>

> **Answer: (b)** — At rest, the table pushes up exactly as hard as gravity pulls down: **10 N**.
</details>

<details>
<summary><b>Q19.</b> The equilibrium rule ΣF = 0 applies to… (a) objects/systems at rest · (b) objects/systems in uniform straight-line motion · (c) both of these · (d) neither</summary>

> **Answer: (c)** — **Both.** "Nothing changing" covers *at rest* **and** *constant velocity*.
</details>

<details>
<summary><b>Q20.</b> The net force on any object in equilibrium is… (a) zero · (b) 10 m/s² · (c) equal to its weight · (d) a bit less than its weight</summary>

> **Answer: (a)** — That's the whole definition: equilibrium means **net force = zero**.
</details>

## 6. 🛠️ Worked Examples (Next-Time Questions)

These four problems take the concepts above and *show them working*. Read the setup, try it, then open the full solution.

### Example 1 — The painter on the scaffold (equilibrium rule)

**Setup:** Two scales read **700 N** (left) and **500 N** (right) where the scaffold ropes attach. Burl the painter weighs **600 N** and carries a **100 N** bucket of paint. **What is the weight of the scaffold?**

```
   700 N ▲                                 ▲ 500 N
         │      🧑‍🎨  (Burl 600 N + 100 N paint)      │
         └────────────[ scaffold ]────────────┘
```

<details>
<summary>▶️ Reveal the solution</summary>

The scaffold is at rest → **equilibrium → ΣF = 0**, so **total up = total down**.

1. **Total upward force** (the two scale readings): 700 N + 500 N = **1200 N**.
2. So the **total downward force (weight) must also be 1200 N**.
3. Burl + paint weigh 600 + 100 = **700 N**.
4. **Scaffold weight** = 1200 − 700 = **500 N**.

> ✅ **Answer: 500 N.** Same move as Concept Check Q17 — "total up = total down."
</details>

---

### Example 2 — The pellet in the spiral tube (inertia & straight-line motion)

**Setup:** A pellet is fired through a flat, coiled (spiral) tube lying on a table — top view. When it shoots out the open end, which path does it follow: **A** (keeps curving), **B** (straight), or **C** (curves the other way)? *Ignore gravity.*

```
        (top view)
        ___________
       /   _____    \
      |   /     \    |
      |  |   →fired   |
       \  \_____/    /
        \__________ EXIT ──► A ?   B ?   C ?
```

<details>
<summary>▶️ Reveal the solution</summary>

**Inside** the tube, the wall pushes the pellet sideways and *forces* it to curve. **The instant it leaves**, no wall, no force — so by the **law of inertia** it must travel in a **straight line**, flying off along the tube's direction at the exit.

> ✅ **Answer: B** — a straight-line path. (A common wrong guess is "A," imagining the pellet "remembers" the curve. It doesn't — nothing keeps curving it.)
</details>

---

### Example 3 — The ball on the swinging string, cut by a razor (combine inertia + gravity)

**Setup:** A heavy ball swings on a string. At the **lowest point** of the swing — where it's moving **horizontally** — a sharp razor instantly **cuts the string**. Which path does the ball take next: **(a)** straight up–down drop, **(b)** a curve that arcs forward and down, or **(c)** straight along its motion then drop?

```
        ╲           ✂ razor cuts here at the bottom
         ╲ string
          ●  ── moving this way ──►   then what?
   ground ─────────────────────────────────
```

<details>
<summary>▶️ Reveal the solution</summary>

Break the motion into two independent parts at the moment of the cut:

1. **Horizontal:** the ball was moving sideways and **no horizontal force** remains → by inertia it keeps that **constant horizontal speed**.
2. **Vertical:** **gravity** now pulls it down, so it **accelerates downward**.

Constant horizontal speed **+** speeding-up downward fall = a smooth curved path called a **parabola**.

> ✅ **Answer: (b)** — a parabolic path (like any projectile). This is inertia (horizontal) and gravity (vertical) acting at the same time.
</details>

---

### Example 4 — Two life preservers in a river (frames of reference)

**Setup:** You float down a swift river. Two life preservers drift with you: one **3 m downstream**, one **3 m upstream**. You can swim to only one first. Which gets you to safety in the **shortest time**: **(a)** the upstream one, **(b)** the downstream one, or **(c)** both take equal time?

```
   ⊚  preserver  (3 m upstream)
   │
   🏊 you            ↑ current carries everything along together
   │
   ⊚  preserver  (3 m downstream)
```

<details>
<summary>▶️ Reveal the solution</summary>

Here's the trick: **you, the water, and both preservers all drift downstream together.** Relative to the *water*, nothing is being swept anywhere — it's just like swimming in a still pool.

In that "pool," both preservers sit the **same distance (3 m)** away. So swimming to either one takes the **same time**. The river's motion carries everyone equally and **cancels out** for the swimmer — exactly the same logic as the jumping-by-a-wall question (Q13): shared motion is undetectable from the inside.

> ✅ **Answer: (c)** — both require the same amount of time.
</details>

## 7. Cheat sheet

| Idea | Say it simply |
|---|---|
| **Inertia** | Matter resists changes in its motion. More mass → more inertia. |
| **Not a force** | Inertia is a *property*, not a push. |
| **Newton's 1st Law** | No net force → rest stays rest, motion stays straight & steady. |
| **Vector** | Has size **and** direction (velocity, force). Speed is *not* a vector. |
| **Net force** | Same way: add. Opposite ways: subtract (points with the bigger one). |
| **Equilibrium** | ΣF = 0 → at rest **or** constant straight-line velocity. |
| **Up = Down trick** | In equilibrium, total upward force = total downward force. |
| **Remove the force** | A curving object flies off in a **straight line**. |
| **Projectile** | Constant horizontal motion + downward gravity = **parabola**. |
| **Shared motion** | If everything moves together, you can't feel it / it cancels out. |
