The Big Idea: Interactions

A force is never lonely. It is always one half of an interaction between two objects.

  • An interaction is between one thing and another.
  • It requires a pair of forces acting on two different objects.
  • Example: your hand and a wall push on each other — you push the wall, the wall pushes you.
Newton's Third Law: Whenever one object exerts a force on a second object, the second object exerts an equal and opposite force on the first.

Shorthand: To every action there is always an opposed, equal reaction.

Identifying Action & Reaction

One force is the action; the partner is the reaction. A simple recipe:

  • Identify the interaction — one thing interacts with another.
  • Action: Object A exerts a force on object B.
  • Reaction: Object B exerts a force on object A.

Action & reaction forces…

  • are co-partners of a single interaction — neither exists without the other.
  • are equal in strength and opposite in direction.
  • always act on different objects — so they never cancel each other out.

Example — Rocket: action = rocket pushes gas backward; reaction = gas pushes rocket forward. ↗ Try it in the Action–Reaction Lab.

Same Force, Different Masses

Action and reaction are always equal forces — but equal forces do not mean equal accelerations.

  • The same force on a small mass produces a large acceleration.
  • The same force on a large mass produces a small acceleration.
Cannon & cannonball feel the same force. The light cannonball rockets away; the heavy cannon barely recoils — because a = F / m.

A bus and a bug feel the same impact force — but the tiny mass of the bug gets an enormous acceleration. ↗ See the Mass & Acceleration sim.

Defining Your System

Whether forces cancel depends on where you draw the system boundary.

  • Orange alone: an external pull (from the apple) accelerates it — Newton's 2nd law.
  • Orange + apple as one system: the apple's pull is now internal; that action–reaction pair cancels, so it causes no acceleration.
  • Add friction from the floor — an external force on the system — and now the system accelerates.

Key rule: only forces that act on the system from outside can accelerate it. Internal action–reaction pairs cancel.

Vectors & Scalars

Vector quantity

  • Has magnitude and direction.
  • Drawn as an arrow.
  • e.g. velocity, force, acceleration.

Scalar quantity

  • Has magnitude only.
  • e.g. mass, volume, speed.

Adding vectors (the Resultant)

  • Same direction → add arithmetically.
  • Opposite directions → subtract arithmetically.
  • At an angle → use the parallelogram rule.
  • At right angles → use the Pythagorean theorem: R² = H² + V².

Run horizontally at 4 m/s through rain falling at 4 m/s → the rain hits you at 45° and about 5.6 m/s. ↗ Build it in the Vector Builder.

Summary — Newton's Three Laws

1st (inertia): An object at rest stays at rest; an object in motion stays in motion at constant speed in a straight line — unless acted on by a net force.
2nd (acceleration): A net force produces acceleration, directly proportional to the force and inversely proportional to the mass. a = F/m.
3rd (action & reaction): Whenever one object exerts a force on a second, the second exerts an equal and opposite force on the first.
